Step 2 · 10 minutes

Case study

The Camera at the Gate

This text was written for this lesson. Riverside University and the people in it are fictional.

Tap a highlighted word to see its meaning.

A

On the first Monday of the spring term, students at Riverside University found a small black camera above the main gate. A notice beside it said: "For your safety, this entrance is under constant surveillance." Nobody had been asked. Nobody had been told what the camera recorded, who would watch the footage or how long it would be retained.

B

By lunchtime, the student union had received forty complaints. Dilnoza Rakhimova, a second-year law student, was among the first to write. "I am not against security," she said. "But a camera that knows my face is not the same as a guard who knows my name. The guard forgets. The software does not." She pointed out that the notice talked about safety but said nothing about consent, and that students had no way to opt out of being filmed if they wanted to attend their classes.

C

The head of security, Mr Tursunov, did not see a problem. Two laptops had been stolen from the library in December, he explained, and a camera was the cheapest deterrent there was. "Anyone with a legitimate purpose on campus has nothing to hide and nothing to fear," he said. "Staff who work late are entitled to feel safe." He also confirmed that the system used facial recognition: every face that passed the gate was compared with a list of people who had been banned from the campus.

D

It was this detail that alarmed the university's data protection officer. In a short memo, she reminded the administration that biometric data is among the most sensitive information a person has. A password can be changed after a data breach; a face cannot. She asked three questions. Who had authorised the system? Was the data encrypted? And was there any independent oversight, or was the security office simply checking itself? "Every measure," she wrote, "has to be proportionate to the risk it addresses. Scanning ten thousand faces to find two thieves casts far too wide a net."

E

The debate soon moved beyond the gate. A philosophy lecturer reminded her students of a question asked by the Roman poet Juvenal almost two thousand years ago: who will guard the guards themselves? In her view, the real issue was not the camera but accountability. A powerful body that watches everyone, but is watched by no one, will sooner or later find a loophole in its own rules. Others feared a slippery slope: today the gate, tomorrow the corridors, and the day after, the exam halls.

F

In the end, the university did not remove the camera, but it did not keep it as it was, either. A committee of staff and students agreed four rules. The footage must be deleted after thirty days. Recordings may be viewed only with written permission. The facial-recognition function must not be used without a court order. And students do not have to give a reason if they ask to see their own file. Dilnoza was only partly satisfied. "We have struck a balance," she said, "but we only struck it because people complained. Next time, the rules should come first and the camera second."
